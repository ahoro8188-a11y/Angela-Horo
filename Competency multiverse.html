The Competency Multiverse

A classroom-based assessment centre simulation, built to observe individual competencies through structured group role-play — part of Competency Mapping and Assessment Centres coursework.

The idea

Assessment centres are how organisations evaluate job-related competencies by watching people act, not just answer — multiple exercises, multiple assessors, live behaviour instead of self-reported claims. Role-play is one of the core tools: a simulated workplace situation that surfaces communication, decision-making, problem-solving, leadership, and adaptability in real time.

The Competency Multiverse recreated that format inside a classroom — five teams, five workplace "universes," one shared rubric, and every participant assessed as an individual regardless of how their team performed as a group.

The five universes

Each team drew a chit deciding which scenario they'd perform, received the situation privately, then had 3 minutes to prepare and 5 minutes to role-play it live:

  • Bigg Boss House — a workplace conflict and disagreement
  • Shark Tank — a business problem requiring an improvement plan
  • The Infinity Shift — multiple urgent problems demanding prioritisation
  • The Loop — a repeated mistake and communication breakdown
  • Mission Impossible — resource constraints forcing an ethical decision

How scoring worked

Every participant was scored individually against five competencies, 5 marks each, for a total of 25:

  • Communication — listening actively, expressing ideas clearly, responding respectfully
  • Decision-making — timely, reasoned, practical choices
  • Problem-solving — identifying the real issue and proposing workable solutions
  • Leadership & teamwork — taking responsibility while involving and supporting others
  • Adaptability under pressure — staying calm, flexible, and constructive when things moved fast

The rubric was behaviour-based by design — built to counter the usual assessment traps: the loudest person in the room isn't automatically the best performer, and every score had to trace back to an observed behaviour, not confidence or fluency.

What we learned

Running and taking part in this activity made a few things concrete rather than theoretical. Assessment centres evaluate behaviour, not credentials or confidence — the same instinct that makes a good interview question different from a good observation exercise. Individual contribution matters independently of team outcome: a team can reach a strong final decision while one or two members did most of the thinking, and a good rubric has to be able to tell the difference.

It also reframed what "leadership" and "communication" actually look like under a structured lens — leadership isn't the person who talks most, it's the person who involves others and keeps the group moving; communication includes listening as much as speaking. And building the rubric itself was as instructive as being scored against it: a common, behaviour-anchored standard is what keeps an assessment fair across five very different rooms, five different scenarios, and multiple assessors — which is exactly the design problem real assessment centres and performance-review systems have to solve.
