Assessment design

The Competency Multiverse

A classroom assessment centre built to observe individual competencies through structured group role-play — part of Competency Mapping and Assessment Centres coursework.

Intent

Assessment centres evaluate job-related competencies by watching people act, not by collecting self-reports. Multiple exercises, multiple assessors, live behaviour. Role-play is a core instrument: a simulated workplace situation that surfaces communication, decision-making, problem-solving, leadership, and adaptability in real time.

The Competency Multiverse recreated that format in a classroom — five teams, five workplace scenarios, one shared rubric, and every participant scored as an individual regardless of how the team performed as a group.

Scenarios

Each team drew a scenario at random, received the brief privately, then had three minutes to prepare and five minutes to perform:

  • Workplace conflict — disagreement requiring resolution without collapsing into dominance.
  • Commercial problem — a business issue requiring a credible improvement plan.
  • Competing urgencies — multiple problems demanding prioritisation under time pressure.
  • Repeated failure — a communication breakdown that had already been allowed to persist.
  • Constrained ethics — scarce resources forcing a defensible decision.

Scoring standard

Every participant was scored individually against five competencies, five marks each, for a total of 25:

  • Communication — listening, clarity, respectful response
  • Decision-making — timely, reasoned, practical choices
  • Problem-solving — identifying the real issue and proposing workable solutions
  • Leadership and teamwork — taking responsibility while involving others
  • Adaptability under pressure — remaining constructive when conditions moved

The rubric was behaviour-anchored by design. Volume was not a proxy for performance. Every mark had to trace to an observed behaviour, not to fluency or confidence.

What the exercise made precise

Assessment centres evaluate behaviour, not credentials. Individual contribution is independent of team outcome: a group can reach a strong decision while two members do most of the thinking, and a competent rubric must be able to tell the difference.

Leadership is not the person who speaks most; it is the person who involves others and keeps the work moving. Communication includes listening. Building the rubric was as instructive as being scored against it: a common, behaviour-anchored standard is what keeps assessment fair across five rooms, five scenarios, and multiple assessors — the same design problem that real assessment centres and performance systems have to solve.
