Beyond the Green Label

Examining the role of Green Human Resource Management (GHRM) in perpetuating — or preventing — corporate greenwashing. A thematic synthesis of 50 academic journals.

The question

Green HR practices — hiring, training, performance management, and rewards designed around environmental goals — are widely assumed to produce genuine sustainability outcomes. Most research treats that link as automatic.

This paper questions that assumption directly: does adopting Green Human Resource Management actually create authentic environmental change, or can it become a polished mechanism for greenwashing — projecting sustainability without the substance behind it?

What the literature shows

Synthesising 50 academic sources, a clear pattern emerged: GHRM's outcomes depend entirely on how it's implemented, not on whether it exists on paper.

Several studies found that green initiatives only translate into real employee behaviour when backed by authentic leadership and system-level integration — not when they're copied from external programmes or run primarily for employer branding. Other research pointed to a recurring risk: organisations adopting environmental rhetoric and policies while HR's actual involvement stays fragmented, passive, or symbolic — leaving performance appraisals, rewards, and job design untouched by the sustainability goals the company claims to hold.

A recurring ethical tension also surfaced across the literature: when bonuses or performance metrics get tied to environmental targets, HR risks prioritising compliance and optics over genuine employee wellbeing and ecological responsibility.

The gaps this paper identified

Despite broad agreement that greenwashing is a real risk, the literature rarely explains how or when GHRM tips from authentic practice into symbolic compliance. Three gaps stood out:

  • Theoretical: no clear framework yet distinguishes symbolic GHRM from substantive GHRM.
  • Empirical: greenwashing itself is rarely measured directly as an outcome of GHRM authenticity — most studies measure performance, not perception or integrity.
  • Methodological: research leans heavily on managerial perspectives and performance metrics, with little space for employees' own read on whether green initiatives feel genuine or performative.

Why it matters

For organisations, the stakes are concrete: sustainability efforts perceived as greenwashing risk reputational damage, employee cynicism, and stakeholder distrust — the opposite of what they're meant to build. For HR specifically, this reframes the function's role: not a passive administrator of green policy, but the decisive actor whose depth of involvement — in leadership authenticity, system integration, and genuine employee empowerment — determines whether a company's environmental story is real change or a well-branded facade.
